CHAPTER I

The morning came in grey and slow, and the old harbour master walked the
length of the quay with his hands behind his back, counting the boats as
he had done every day for thirty years without ever once finding a number
he liked.

“Is it true, Mr. Hale, that the café on the hill has closed?” asked the
girl—and then, because he said nothing, she asked it again, more softly,
as though the question might be _kinder_ the second time.

In the spring of 1884 the harbour board voted to widen the channel and the
fishermen, who had never been asked about anything in their lives, said that
they would believe it when they saw it.

The captain called it a zzbadword of the first order, and the crew, who had
heard the captain call many things many names over the years, only nodded
and went on coiling the rope.

It rained.

The lamp on the north pier had not been lit since the storm, and nobody in
the village could say who ought to light it. The children dared one another
to climb the iron steps, though none of them ever reached the top.

length of the quay with his hands behind his back, counting the boats as
